The demo works. The model is accurate on the test set, the dashboard is clean and the leadership team likes it. Eighteen months later the project is still a pilot. This pattern is common enough that it deserves a name, and it is rarely about the model.

{% include figure.html chart="hbar" tone="free" title="Where technology projects stop" subtitle="Share of projects reaching each stage, from idea to production" %}

## The model is the easy part

Building a model or tool that performs well in a controlled test is now the easy part for most business and administrative use cases. What is not solved is everything around it.

- **Procurement.** Pilots are often bought as services or research grants. Moving to production means a new tender, a new budget line and a new owner.
- **Data rights.** The pilot used a data extract shared under a one-off agreement. Production needs a standing legal basis, and nobody drafted one.
- **Accountability.** When the system makes a wrong call, who answers for it? If no manager is willing to own that, the system will not be switched on.

## Design for production on day one

Projects that do reach production tend to answer three questions before the pilot starts: who will own the system in year three, under what legal basis the data will flow, and which existing decision the system replaces. If those answers are vague, the pilot is a demonstration, not a step towards deployment.

{% include figure.html chart="dots" tone="free" title="Ambition and adoption" subtitle="Pilot budget against months to production, by project" %}

The question to ask of any pilot is not "does it work?" It is "who will be running this when the consultants leave?"
