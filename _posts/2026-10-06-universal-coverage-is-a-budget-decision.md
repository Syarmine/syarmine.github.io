Every government says it wants universal health coverage. Far fewer can say what they are willing to stop paying for to get there. That second question is where coverage is actually decided, and it is rarely answered in the health ministry.

## Coverage has three dimensions, and money moves all of them

Coverage is usually described as a cube: how many people are covered, which services are included, and how much of the cost is covered. Each face of that cube has a price. Adding people costs money. Adding services costs money. Reducing what patients pay at the point of care costs money. A health ministry can design a benefit package. It cannot fund one on its own.

## The binding constraint is fiscal space

When coverage stalls, the usual diagnosis is weak primary care, poor targeting or low enrolment. Those are real. But the more common cause is simpler: the budget envelope did not grow, so the package could not either. Fiscal space for health comes from economic growth, new revenue, reprioritisation within the budget, or efficiency gains. Only the last is fully within a health ministry's control, and it is the slowest.

## What this means for reform

Three implications follow.

1. **Cost the package before announcing it.** A benefit package without a multi-year costing is a promise, not a policy.
2. **Make the trade-offs explicit.** Every new inclusion should name what it displaces or how it is paid for.
3. **Bring the finance ministry in early.** Reforms that treat the treasury as an audience rather than a co-owner tend to be cut at the first budget review.

Coverage is a health goal. Getting there is a budget negotiation, and it should be run like one.
