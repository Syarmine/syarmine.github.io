Most strategy documents list more priorities than the organisation can pay for. That is not a strategy. A strategy is the set of choices about what to fund, what to stop funding and what to leave alone, and those choices are made in the budget, not the slide deck.

## Priorities have a price

Every priority competes for the same three things: money, people's time and leadership attention. When a new priority is announced without saying which existing work it displaces, one of two things happens. Either it quietly gets no resources, or it takes them from something else without anyone deciding that it should.

## The room to choose is smaller than it looks

Most of any budget is already spoken for: salaries, contracts, obligations made in earlier years. The share that is genuinely open to choice each year is often small. That is where strategy actually happens, and it is why reallocation, not new money, is the main lever most leaders have.

## What this means in practice

1. **Cost the plan before announcing it.** A priority without a multi-year cost is a promise, not a plan.
2. **Name what stops.** Every new commitment should say what it displaces or how it is paid for.
3. **Bring finance in early.** Plans that treat the finance team as an audience rather than a co-owner tend to be cut at the first review.
